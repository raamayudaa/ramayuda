Truthfully, the fact that I never once thought to count how many times this had happened has been my greatest oversight so far.

Somewhere along the way—I can no longer remember when—this routine began to shadow my mornings and my mother's kitchen at home. The distance between leaving the house and returning became a stretch of time I hardly ever noticed. And yet, it is precisely these ordinary mornings that slowly seep into memory, spreading quietly until they capture the silhouette of my mother's routines, eventually becoming habits I pass through without noticing, much like the many conversations in our house that ended before they ever became complete sentences.

Yes, this is not merely about a cup of instant spaghetti this morning.

I would rather call everything surrounding our dining table a pattern that has repeated itself for years. A pattern in which a ready-made cup of spaghetti, bought on the way home after dropping the neighbour's child off at school, is enough to accompany me through an entire day. A pattern in which the motorcycle keys are always left in exactly the same place beside everything else, as though the house has long since settled into an order of its own.

At that table, I have lost count of how many plates of tofu and tempeh I have passed without truly seeing them. I have tasted every side dish my mother bought for me, yet hardly ever turned to look at the plate she prepared for herself.

Additional note: By my own estimate, this has happened around 880 times—almost certainly more. Yet only this morning was it recorded as an observation. Its significance has yet to be verified.

Perhaps this is simply how memory begins to work as one grows older.

Little by little, we stop remembering what our parents bought for us, only to find ourselves remembering what they never bought for themselves.

It is as though, after spending so many years living simply as someone's child, we finally learn to read the footnotes that had quietly been holding the entire story together—footnotes rewritten every day, on the same plate, with the same modest meal.

I imagine that, had I looked a little sooner, I would have found something that had always been there. And because it had always been there, it never seemed worthy of being written down. It is like writing a date on a pane of glass, believing it will preserve a moment forever, only to find the glass perfectly clean ten years later, as though nothing had ever been written there at all.

The difference is that my mother's plate was never empty.

It was simply too full of the same familiar routine for me to think it deserved my attention.

Perhaps tomorrow morning will be exactly the same.

Perhaps my mother herself has forgotten how many times this has happened, just as most repetitions are destined to lose the feeling of ever having been an event.

After thinking about all the mornings I allowed to pass unnoticed, I can think of no better time than tonight to pause for a while and leave behind a few words about this day before I leave for a city that belongs to someone else.

Mother remains well, through every morning that quietly repeats itself.
